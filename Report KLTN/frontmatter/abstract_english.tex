\begin{center}
{\fontsize{16pt}{19.2pt}\selectfont \textbf{BUILDING A REINFORCEMENT LEARNING MODEL FOR OPTIMIZING REORDER POLICY IN MULTI-WAREHOUSE INVENTORY MANAGEMENT}\par}
\vspace{0.5cm}
{\fontsize{16pt}{19.2pt}\selectfont \textbf{ABSTRACT}\par}
\end{center}
\vspace{0.2cm}

Inventory control across a retail network of multiple warehouses and multiple
stock-keeping units (SKUs) is a sequential decision-making problem under
uncertainty that requires balancing several conflicting cost components at
once: holding cost, stockout cost, fixed ordering cost, warehouse overflow
penalty, and the requirement to sustain a target customer service level.
Classical policies such as EOQ, $(s, S)$ and the Newsvendor model rest on
restrictive assumptions --- deterministic or stationary demand, fixed lead
times, and unconstrained storage capacity --- and therefore lose their
optimality guarantees once demand is stochastic, lead times fluctuate, and
multiple SKUs must share a finite warehouse capacity.

This thesis addresses the problem through multi-agent reinforcement learning.
A system of $N = 10$ warehouses and $M = 30$ representative SKUs is modelled as
$300$ agents, one per warehouse--SKU pair, each issuing its own replenishment
decisions from local observations. The agents are trained with Independent
Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (IPPO) under full parameter sharing:
a single set of Actor--Critic weights is shared across all $300$ agents, which
keeps the number of learnable parameters constant as the system scales. The
reward is designed with local decomposition, a 30-day rolling-window fill-rate
penalty term, and a per-pair normalization mechanism that reduces the variance
of the learning signal across SKUs whose cost scales differ by several orders
of magnitude.

The simulation environment follows the Gymnasium interface, with demand and
price parameters calibrated from the real-world M5 Walmart retail dataset (10
stores, 30 representative SKUs, 1,941 days of sales data) and stochastic lead
times drawn from one to three days. The proposed model is benchmarked against
three classical inventory policies (EOQ, $(s, S)$, Newsvendor), each tuned by
grid search on the same environment, under a unified evaluation protocol
involving multiple random seeds and statistical significance testing, across
four metric groups: total operational cost, fill rate, and the breakdown of
individual cost components.

Experimental results show that, when compared at a matched minimum service
level, IPPO achieves the lowest total operational cost among all four methods
-- $47.5\%$ cheaper than EOQ, $21.2\%$ cheaper than $(s, S)$, and $11.3\%$
cheaper than the Newsvendor policy -- whereas an unmatched direct comparison
misleadingly suggests IPPO is more expensive than EOQ. The shared-parameter
policy generalizes well to medium- and high-demand SKUs but does not fully
close the service-level gap for the lowest-demand, most intermittent SKUs. The
thesis also reports, in full honesty, the scenarios in which the proposed
method does not outperform the classical baselines, together with the study's
limitations and directions for future work.
